Identifying which training images most influence a given test view in a 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction, and in which direction, without retraining the model.

Ch. 1 · Overview

Tracing a reconstruction back to its training images

A 3D Gaussian Splatting model reconstructs a scene from multiple input images captured from overlapping viewpoints, and it does not depend on every one of them equally. Quantifying that contribution is the aim of data attribution, the problem of tracing an output back to the training images most responsible for it.

The direct way to measure it is to remove an image, retrain, and observe the change, but this LOO retraining is prohibitively expensive. Influence functions estimate the same effect without retraining, by combining training and test gradients through the inverse Hessian of the loss. This thesis estimates influence with a diagonal empirical Fisher approximation and validates the scores against LOO retraining under the model's own training loss, across three Mip-NeRF 360 scenes.

Ch. 1 · Research questions

The question, and two hypotheses

RQ

Can influence functions be used to identify both beneficial and harmful training images in 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructions, and do they capture something that simpler geometric measures do not?

H1

Influence functions can quantify the individual contribution of each training image to the final reconstruction, identifying both beneficial and harmful views.

H2

Influence scores approximate the LOO effect of each training image at a fraction of the retraining cost.

Ch. 2 & 3 · Background and related work

Data attribution and Radiance Fields

Data attribution methods exist, but they do not transfer directly to the 3DGS setting.

✗

PruNeRF

Uses influence functions to prune distractors, but in NeRF. Its MLP backbone satisfies the layer-wise structure the K-FAC approximation requires. Gaussian attributes do not.

✗

FisherRF / PUP-3DGS

Fisher information is already computed in 3DGS, but for view selection (FisherRF, colors) and pruning (PUP-3DGS, means and scales).

✗

TracIn

A popular gradient-based alternative. Needs checkpoints across training, but densification changes the number of Gaussians, so checkpoints share no common parameter space.

The colors and m+s parameter groups evaluated later come from these two works.

How this work differs

Both Fisher-based methods assume a Gaussian distribution over rendered pixel values, under which the negative log-likelihood reduces to an MSE objective and the Fisher coincides with the Gauss-Newton Hessian. Their curvature is therefore derived from a loss the model was never trained on. Here the empirical Fisher is computed directly from the L1 and D-SSIM training loss, the same loss the LOO deltas are measured under.

On ground truth: influence functions may approximate the PBRF more accurately than they approximate LOO, but the PBRF is itself an approximation of that removal effect, and LOO measures it directly.

To the best of my knowledge, this is the first work to apply influence functions in 3DGS and validate them against LOO retraining.
Ch. 4 · Method

Estimating influence, and the ground truth it is validated against

The predicted change in a test view's loss when a training image is removed takes the classical inverse-Hessian form. The LOO delta is the same quantity measured rather than estimated.

\[ \mathcal{I}(z_{\text{train}}, z_{\text{test}}) = \frac{1}{N}\, \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}})^{\top}\, H^{-1}\, \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(z_{\text{train}}) \]
\[ \Delta\mathcal{L}(k, j) \;=\; \mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{j};\, \hat{\theta}_{-k}) \;-\; \mathcal{L}(z^{*}_{j};\, \hat{\theta}) \]

The three factors of the estimate:

∇θℒ(ztest)
test gradient: how this view's loss responds to each parameter
H−1
curvature: how far the parameters actually move
∇θℒ(ztrain)
training gradient: how this image pushed them during training

The estimate is a tangent. Retraining traces the true curve; the influence function reads its slope at the trained model and extrapolates to \(\epsilon = -1/N\), which is removal. The gap between the two is the linearization error, and it is why a large score is more trustworthy than a small one.

A positive delta means removing the image raised the loss, so the image was beneficial; a negative delta means it was harmful. Agreement is reported as Pearson rP and Spearman rS. Spearman is the one that matters here, since the question is which images rank highest.

Why this approximation

The inverse Hessian is intractable at millions of Gaussian parameters, and the choice of what replaces it is forced more than preferred. The rasterizer provides a first-order backward pass and nothing else, which rules out most of the standard machinery.

✗ CG / LiSSA\(Hv\) Why not Needs Hessian vector products, which require differentiating through the loss a second time. The rasterizer has no second backward pass.
✗ Gauss-Newton\(J^{\top}J\) Why not Needs Jacobian vector products of the rendered output. Prior second order work in 3DGS had to write custom CUDA kernels to get them.
✗ EK-FAC Why not Requires the layered structure of a multilayer perceptron. Gaussian attributes are explicit parameters, not weights passed through layers.
✗ True Fisher\(F = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}^{\top}\right]\) Why not Takes an expectation over labels drawn from the model’s own distribution, which is undefined for a loss that is not a negative log likelihood.
✓ Empirical Fisher\(F = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k \nabla\mathcal{L}(z_k)\,\nabla\mathcal{L}(z_k)^{\top}\) Why this one Positive semi-definite by construction for any loss, the same gradient outer product form as the Gauss-Newton Hessian at the image level, and computable from the per image loss gradients that already exist.
Still very large, so it is further restricted to the diagonal \( \tilde{F}_{ii} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\left[\nabla\mathcal{L}(z_k)_i\right]^{2} \)
The influence estimate used in this thesis
\[ \mathcal{I}(z_{\text{train}}, z_{\text{test}}) \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{P} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(z_{\text{test}})}{\partial \theta_i}\; \tilde{F}^{-1}_{ii}\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(z_{\text{train}})}{\partial \theta_i} \]
know more

Rather than damping the Fisher, near-zero curvature entries are dropped below a threshold (α = 10⁻³ of the group mean), which removes 19% to 45% of the parameters per group yet preserves more than 99.99% of the diagonal Fisher mass.

All six Gaussian attribute groups are tracked during the Fisher pass and ablated afterwards. Unless stated otherwise the default is means, for its low parameter count (∼2.4M in Garden against ∼47M for all six) and its competitive performance with the larger groups on most views.

The empirical Fisher is not theoretically guaranteed to be a good approximation. So it is validated empirically: per test view against the LOO loss deltas, and against two geometric measures, camera-pose distance and Gaussian overlap.

But can either substitute influence? They are conceptually simpler and computationally cheaper, so it is worth assessing whether the training images each of them identifies as most beneficial and most harmful coincide with those influence identifies. If they point to the same images, there is no practical value in computing influence.

Do the measures select the same training images?

The comparison is made on Garden test view 14, the only evaluated view whose selected set contains training images with large negative influence estimates.

Beneficial sideMostly agreement

On three of the five, 111, 112 and 139. A training image whose camera is close to the test view usually shares content with it, and shared content usually helps the reconstruction, so proximity alone orders the beneficial images about as well as influence does.

Harmful sideNo agreement

This is what separates the measures. Influence carries a sign, and on this view the sign is correct: train 85, which only influence marks as harmful, is confirmed harmful by the LOO retraining.

Computational cost

Computing the eFIM influence for a whole scene is computationally inexpensive. Garden needs one gradient pass over the training images to form the Fisher diagonal and then one gradient per test view, after which each score is a cheap combination of the stored gradients. Storing the gradients instead of recomputing them is what makes each score cheap, and it is also what sets the memory requirement: all training image gradients are held at once, peaking at about 35 GB.

~13 min eFIM influence, all six parameter groups × all six Garden test views.
~56 min One single LOO retrain, a full Splatfacto run of 30,000 iterations, median run time.
~156 h Full attribution by retraining, over Garden’s 167 training images.

Ch. 6 · Conclusions

Influence functions can identify both beneficial and harmful images under specific conditions

Two hypotheses were formulated: that influence functions can quantify each training image’s individual contribution to the final reconstruction (H1), and that influence estimates approximate the LOO effect of each training image at a fraction of the retraining cost (H2).

On the research question and H1

Influence functions can identify beneficial and harmful training images, under two conditions.

Condition 1 Only among the training images the estimate itself ranks most influential.
Condition 2 Only when computed on the appropriate parameter group. The best group still changes from view to view, and this thesis found no rule to find it a priori.
On H2

The estimates approximate the LOO effect at a small fraction of the retraining cost, correlating moderately to strongly with it and reaching rS = +0.92 on Garden test view 14. H2 holds.

Influence versus geometry

On the images that help a view, influence is arguably not needed. For harmful image detection it remains an open question, with a slight advantage to influence, since it is gradient based, and the LOO retraining corroborated it on at least one evaluated test view.
